Safe claims
Central paradox

AI may be helping keep aggregate GDP, corporate investment, and data-center construction strong enough to avoid recession-style macro readings, while simultaneously revealing how the burden of high interest rates falls hardest on households, housing, and smaller borrowers.

Evidence supports a qualified version

Best framing: monetary policy is not broken, but transmission is increasingly uneven across sectors and balance sheets.

Complications

Counterarguments worth keeping

The app keeps the strongest disconfirming evidence visible so the final article does not overstate the case.

The boom may be overstated.

Company capex is not identical to U.S.-located AI infrastructure spending, and reported totals include non-AI and overseas investment.

Rates still matter to Big Tech.

Oracle, Amazon, leases, bonds, and project finance all show that capital costs have not disappeared; they are just distributed differently.

AI may turn disinflationary later.

Productivity gains could eventually offset current buildout pressure if output scales faster than demand and financing costs.

Housing pain has many causes.

Mortgage rates, supply shortages, insurance, taxes, and local zoning matter more directly than AI in the current affordability crisis.

Drafting guardrails

What we can safely say

These lines are phrased to survive fact-checking and keep the argument rigorous rather than rhetorical.

Well supported

The Fed moves one rate, but that rate does not land equally across the economy. Households and small firms face retail borrowing conditions directly, while hyperscalers finance through internal cash, bonds, leases, and project structures.

Plausible with care

AI investment may be shifting more of monetary restraint onto housing and smaller borrowers by remaining unusually resilient during tight-money conditions.

Avoid

Do not say monetary policy is broken, that all capex is AI, or that AI is the primary cause of national cost-of-living stress.
